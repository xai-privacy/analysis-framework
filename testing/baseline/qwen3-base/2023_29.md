## Original question

When a principal delegates tasks to an agent, but cannot perfectly observe the agent’s actions, the agent may behave in ways that maximize their own interests at the expense of the principal’s interests. This is called the principal-agent problem. There is an argument that “the principal-agent problem also arises for homeowners who sell their house through real estate agents.” In the United States, real estate agents usually receive a 6% commission on the sale price, but after other participants take their share, the agent effectively receives only about 1.5%. Since the commission is linked to the sale price, it may seem the agent is incentivized to sell the house at the highest possible price. However, this is only true to a limited extent. For example, if the sale price is increased by $10,000, the agent only earns an additional $150. In order to obtain this higher price, the property would need to stay on the market longer, incurring costs for advertising and showing the home to potential buyers. Therefore, rather than paying these extra costs for an additional $150, the agent is incentivized to sell the house at a reasonable price relatively quickly.
