## Original Rationale

**Preliminaries**

The given ⓐ can be interpreted as the so-called 'defection rate.' By analyzing this defection rate based on the election results, we can determine which factor—policy or candidate characteristics—had a relatively greater influence in that election. If an election is expected to bring about a greater change in policy, as the `<hypothesis>` claims, voters are more likely to cast their ballots according to policy factors rather than candidate characteristic factors, so it can be inferred that the defection rate will be relatively low.

**Statement (a) Rationale**

The president has greater policy influence than individual parliament members. When the presidential and parliamentary elections are held simultaneously, the outcome of the presidential election is expected to have a greater impact on future policies than the parliamentary election. Therefore, the defection rate in the parliamentary election would be predicted to be higher. If the election results are consistent with this, the `<hypothesis>` would be strengthened. Thus, (a) is a valid inference.

**Statement (b) Rationale**

In a parliamentary election, party supporters would be more likely to seek policy change when the sitting president is from an opposing party rather than from their own preferred party. This means that progressive party supporters are more likely to believe that the parliamentary election has a greater policy impact when the president is from a conservative party than when the president is from a progressive party. According to the `<hypothesis>`, the defection rate among progressive party supporters would be lower when the president is from a conservative party than when the president is from a progressive party. Therefore, if this election result were observed, it would strengthen the `<hypothesis>`. However, without knowing whether progressive party supporters actually consider the parliamentary election to have a greater policy impact when the president is from a conservative party versus a progressive party, we cannot infer whether the defection rate being lower strengthens or weakens the `<hypothesis>`. Thus, (b) is not a valid inference.

**Statement (c) Rationale**

In a parliamentary election, there is a lower likelihood of policy change when the majority party in parliament does not change compared to when it does. Therefore, when the majority party does not change, the parliamentary election is less likely to have a significant policy impact. According to the `<hypothesis>`, the defection rate should be higher when the majority party does not change. If the defection rate was actually lower in such a scenario, the `<hypothesis>` would be weakened. Even if this is not conclusive, one cannot infer that the `<hypothesis>` is strengthened. Thus, (c) is not a valid inference.

**Answer**

Only (a) is a correct inference from the options given, so the answer is ①.

> According to the rationale, only statement (a) is correct.
