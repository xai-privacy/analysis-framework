## 2. LEET-Arg Question

```text
An artist created an artwork by placing an intricately crafted artificial flower inside a transparent cubic acrylic box with sides about 1 meter long, and installing a fluorescent light on one face. He named the work "ⓐ Two Types of Permanence" and sold it to an art museum. He instructed the museum to keep the piece always connected to power, but that when the fluorescent light eventually reached the end of its lifespan, it should be left as is and not replaced. Years later, the fluorescent light finally ceased to function. Judging it important to display the work in its original appearance, the museum suggested to the artist that the light should be replaced with a new one whenever it failed. Although the artist strongly objected, he eventually agreed to allow the light to be replaced, out of concern that refusing the museum's request might make it difficult to display and sell works in the future. A debate arose among critics regarding the impact of the artist's approval on the identity and meaning of the piece. Alice: Since replacing the fluorescent light is based on the artist's approval, this change does not alter the identity of the work. However, because this is a change in an essential attribute of the work, the work should now be seen as having only a superficial meaning, unlike before. Bob: The artist only approved the alteration of the work’s physical attribute due to the museum's pressure. This act of approval does not reflect the artist's true intention. Since the identity of the work lies in the artist’s true intention, even if the work’s physical attribute has changed, its identity remains the same as before, and thus its meaning has also not changed. Charlie: The work was complete at the moment it was made public, and any subsequent act of approval cannot alter its identity—even if that approval aligns with the artist's true intentions. <question> Which of the following analyses regarding the above debate is/are correct? Select all correct options from <statements> as they appear. <statements> (a) According to Alice, a change in some physical attribute of the work can alter its meaning. (b) According to Charlie, an act of approval by the creator after the fact has the same effect as the physical act of making the work at the time of creation. (c) If the museum secretly replaced the fluorescent light of ⓐ with a new one without the creator's knowledge, both Bob and Charlie agree that the meaning of ⓐ could change. <choices> ① (a) ② (c) ③ (a), (b) ④ (b), (c) ⑤ (a), (b), (c)
```

## 3. Gold Answer and Original Rationale

```text
Gold answer: 1
```

**preliminaries:** Alice claims that as long as a change in the work’s attribute occurred with the creator’s approval, it may not damage the identity of the work, yet she also asserts that in this case there has been a change in meaning. On the other hand, Bob asserts that the identity and meaning of the work depend on the artist’s true intention, and that if the act of approval does not reflect the artist's true intention, it cannot affect the work’s identity or meaning. Charlie believes that the identity of the work is completed at the moment it is made public, and that subsequent changes in physical attributes or acts of approval have no effect on the work’s identity.

**statement_1_rationale:** (a) Alice sees that the change of attribute from “the fluorescent light has reached the end of its lifespan” to “the fluorescent light is continuously functioning” changes the meaning of the work. Thus, Alice does believe that a change in a physical attribute of the work can alter its meaning. (a) is a correct analysis.

**statement_2_rationale:** (b) If an act of approval by the creator after the fact had the same effect as the act of physically creating the work, then post-approval could change its attributes, identity, or meaning. This contradicts Charlie’s view, so (b) is an incorrect analysis.

**statement_3_rationale:** (c) If the museum replaced the light in ⓐ with a new one without the artist’s knowledge, then it would be a change in physical attribute independent of the artist's actual intention. Since Bob claims that the identity and meaning depend on the artist’s actual intention, he would not agree that such unilateral action by the museum could change the meaning of ⓐ. Thus, (c) is an incorrect analysis.

**answer:** Since only (a) is the correct analysis in the options, the answer is ①.

## 14. Argumentation Framework Result Analysis

- **Predicted answer:** 1
- **Gold answer:** 1
- **Correct or incorrect:** Correct.
- **Did argument extraction help?** Yes. The argument extraction step correctly separated the three positions: Alice says the approved replacement preserves identity but changes meaning; Bob says pressured approval does not reflect true intention and therefore identity/meaning remain unchanged; Charlie says the work's identity was fixed when made public and later approval cannot alter it.
- **Did attack-relation extraction help?** Only partially. The attack graph helped keep the three positions visible, but several edges were conceptually weak or confused. For example, Bob and Charlie are not simply attacking each other about approval; they both resist the idea that the later replacement changes the work's meaning, although they do so for different reasons.
- **Main remaining issue:** The final answer is correct, but the model still did not follow the required exact final format `Answer: <number>`. It wrote `① (a),` instead. The reasoning is usable, but the format remains non-compliant for automatic parsing.

## 15. Baseline vs Argumentation Framework Comparison

- **Baseline outcome:** Incorrect / ambiguous. The baseline identified (a) correctly but also treated (c) as correct and gave a malformed final answer.
- **Argumentation-framework outcome:** Correct. The argumentation-framework response selected only (a), which matches the gold answer.
- **Improved / worse / inconclusive:** Improved.

The argumentation-framework sequence improved performance on this question because the extracted arguments made the disagreement between Alice, Bob, and Charlie clearer. In particular, Prompt 1 correctly captured that Alice allows a change in meaning, while Bob and Charlie deny that the replacement changes identity or meaning. The attack-relation step was not fully reliable, because the graph included weak or misleading edges, but the final evaluation still used the extracted positions more successfully than the baseline. Overall, this is a positive example where decomposing the problem into argument extraction and statement evaluation helped Llama 3.2 3B reach the correct answer despite imperfections in the intermediate graph.

## 16. Final Notes

This example suggests that the three-step prompting approach can help small local models when the question turns on distinguishing each speaker's theory of identity and meaning. However, the attack-graph step remains noisy: the model produced edges that sound formal but do not always reflect genuine attacks. A useful prompt improvement would be to ask the model to distinguish **agreement**, **disagreement**, and **partial disagreement** before forcing everything into attack edges. This case also shows that final-answer formatting still needs improvement, because the model reached the correct substantive answer but did not write the exact required line `Answer: 1`.
