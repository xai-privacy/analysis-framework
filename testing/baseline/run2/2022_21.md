## Original Question

When we use the name “Napoleon,” which is the name of a historical figure, in everyday life, this name refers to the real person, Napoleon. However, in fictional works such as the novel War and Peace, in which Napoleon appears as a character, does the name “Napoleon” used there also refer to the real person Napoleon? We naturally have the intuition that it does.But let us imagine an extreme fictional work in which Napoleon appears as an Americano and goes on adventures with coffee friends. Since the Napoleon appearing here bears no resemblance whatsoever to the real person Napoleon, we can conclude that the name “Napoleon” used in this work simply refers to a character in fiction, not the real historical figure.Thus, if we accept the claim that at least in certain works of fiction, the name “Napoleon” does not refer to the actual person, we must accept one of the following two:⑴ In some works of fiction, the name “Napoleon” does not refer to the real person, but in other works of fiction, the name “Napoleon” does refer to the real person.⑵ In all works of fiction, the name “Napoleon” does not refer to the real person.Now, considering theoretical simplicity and unity, unless there is a serious problem with the view in ⑵, we should adopt ⑵ over ⑴. One might think that the intuition that the “Napoleon” used in War and Peace refers to the real Napoleon is problematic and clashes with ⑵, but this can be explained as follows: The “Napoleon” used in War and Peace also refers to a character within the fiction, and because this fictional character Napoleon has features similar to the real historical figure Napoleon, we mistakenly have the intuition that the name refers to the real person. <question> Which of the following analyses of the above passage is/are correct?
