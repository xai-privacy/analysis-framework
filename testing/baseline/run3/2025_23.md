## Original Question

Alice: The proposition expressed by a sentence is the set of possible worlds in which that sentence is true. For instance, the sentence "3 is odd or not odd" expresses the proposition which is the set of all possible worlds. Bob: A proposition is a kind of ordered pair. For example, the proposition expressed by the sentence "3 is odd" can be understood as an ordered pair composed of the object referred to by "3" and the property referred to by "is odd." If we let the object referred to by "3" be m and the property referred to by "is odd" be F, then the proposition expressed by "3 is odd" is <m, F>. As another example, the sentence "Suchul loves Heeyoung" expresses a proposition that can be understood as an ordered triplet composed of the object referred to by "Suchul," the object referred to by "Heeyoung," and the binary relation referred to by "loves." Both Alice and Bob would agree that, provided "Suchul" and "Heeyoung" refer to different objects, the sentences "Suchul loves Heeyoung" and "Heeyoung loves Suchul" express different propositions. (b). A person who believes that the sentences "A round square exists" and "3 is odd and not odd" have different meanings would oppose Alice's view. (c). If "Morning Star" and "Evening Star" refer to the same object, then someone who believes that the sentences "The Morning Star is beautiful" and "The Evening Star is beautiful" have different meanings would oppose Bob's view. <choices> ① (b) ② (c) ③ (a), (b) ④ (a), (c) ⑤ (a), (b), (c)

All of (a), (b), and (c) are valid inferences, so the answer is ⑤.
