## 2. LEET-Arg Question

```text
The various factors influencing a voter’s choice in an election can be broadly divided into ‘policy factors’ and ‘candidate characteristic factors.’ Policy factors are those related to a voter’s policy preferences or ideological tendencies. Progressive voters tend to vote for progressive parties, while conservative voters tend to support conservative parties; this is due to policy factors. Candidate characteristic factors, on the other hand, refer to personal characteristics such as educational background, career experience, and appearance, independent of policy factors. When a candidate from a preferred party is also superior in terms of candidate characteristic factors, the voter’s choice is obvious. However, when the two factors conflict, it is possible for a progressive voter to vote for a conservative party’s candidate, or vice versa. Regarding the relative influence of policy factors and candidate characteristic factors, the following hypothesis is proposed: <hypothesis> In elections where the impact on policy is greater, the relative influence of policy factors is greater than that of candidate characteristic factors. To test this hypothesis, a survey was conducted among individual party supporters, measuring “ⓐ among those who answered that the candidate from a party they do not support is superior in personal characteristics compared to their preferred party’s candidate, the proportion who actually voted for the superior candidate.” In general, the president has a greater impact on policy than individual members of parliament. <question> From the following, which of the statements in <statements> can be inferred correctly? <statements> (a) When the presidential and parliamentary elections were held simultaneously, if ⓐ was higher in the parliamentary election than in the presidential election, this would strengthen the <hypothesis>. (b) If, in the parliamentary election, ⓐ among progressive party supporters was lower when the president was from a conservative party compared to when the president was from a progressive party, then the <hypothesis> would be weakened. (c) If, in the parliamentary election, ⓐ was lower when the majority party in parliament did not change compared to when it did change, this would strengthen the <hypothesis>. <choices> ① (a) ② (b) ③ (a), (c) ④ (b), (c) ⑤ (a), (b), (c)
```

## 3. Gold Answer and Original Rationale

```text
Gold answer: 1
```

**preliminaries:** The given ⓐ can be interpreted as the so-called ‘defection rate.’ By analyzing this defection rate based on the election results, we can determine which factor—policy or candidate characteristics—had a relatively greater influence in that election. If an election is expected to bring about a greater change in policy, as the <hypothesis> claims, voters are more likely to cast their ballots according to policy factors rather than candidate characteristic factors, so it can be inferred that the defection rate will be relatively low.

**statement_1_rationale:** (a) The president has greater policy influence than individual parliament members. When the presidential and parliamentary elections are held simultaneously, the outcome of the presidential election is expected to have a greater impact on future policies than the parliamentary election. Therefore, the defection rate in the parliamentary election would be predicted to be higher. If the election results are consistent with this, the <hypothesis> would be strengthened. Thus, (a) is a valid inference.

**statement_2_rationale:** (b) In a parliamentary election, party supporters would be more likely to seek policy change when the sitting president is from an opposing party rather than from their own preferred party. This means that progressive party supporters are more likely to believe that the parliamentary election has a greater policy impact when the president is from a conservative party than when the president is from a progressive party. According to the <hypothesis>, the defection rate among progressive party supporters would be lower when the president is from a conservative party than when the president is from a progressive party. Therefore, if this election result were observed, it would strengthen the <hypothesis>. However, without knowing whether progressive party supporters actually consider the parliamentary election to have a greater policy impact when the president is from a conservative party versus a progressive party, we cannot infer whether the defection rate being lower strengthens or weakens the <hypothesis>. Thus, (b) is not a valid inference.

**statement_3_rationale:** (c) In a parliamentary election, there is a lower likelihood of policy change when the majority party in parliament does not change compared to when it does. Therefore, when the majority party does not change, the parliamentary election is less likely to have a significant policy impact. According to the <hypothesis>, the defection rate should be higher when the majority party does not change. If the defection rate was actually lower in such a scenario, the <hypothesis> would be weakened. Even if this is not conclusive, one cannot infer that the <hypothesis> is strengthened. Thus, (c) is not a valid inference.

**answer:** Only (a) is a correct inference from the options given, so the answer is ①.

## 14. Argumentation Framework Result Analysis

- **Predicted answer:** 2 (`② (b)`)
- **Gold answer:** 1 (`① (a)`)
- **Correct or incorrect:** Incorrect
- **Did argument extraction help?** Only partially. The extraction captured the broad distinction between policy factors and candidate-characteristic factors, but it did not clearly represent the key operational variable ⓐ as a defection rate. Without that, the later reasoning could not correctly evaluate which results strengthen or weaken the hypothesis.
- **Did attack-relation extraction help?** No. The attack graph converted the statistical/hypothesis-testing problem into a misleading A → B → C argument chain. This did not clarify the relation between policy impact, defection rate, and the three answer statements.
- **Main remaining issue:** The model failed to connect the direction of ⓐ to the hypothesis. It treated a lower defection rate as evidence for candidate-characteristic influence in some places and policy-factor influence in others, leading to an incorrect final answer. The final answer also did not follow the required exact format `Answer: <number>`.

## 15. Baseline vs Argumentation Framework Comparison

- **Baseline outcome:** Incorrect — predicted `④ (b), (c)` instead of `① (a)`.
- **Argumentation-framework outcome:** Incorrect — predicted `② (b)` instead of `① (a)`.
- **Improved / worse / inconclusive:** Inconclusive/slightly worse.

The argumentation-framework approach did not help on this question. The baseline already misunderstood the central variable ⓐ, but the argumentation prompt carried that misunderstanding forward instead of correcting it. The extracted arguments were too abstract and did not explicitly state the key inference: if policy impact is greater, policy voting should dominate, so the defection rate ⓐ should be lower. The attack-relation step made the reasoning worse by forcing a graph structure onto a problem that is really about experimental interpretation and directionality of evidence.

## 16. Final Notes

This question shows a limitation of the current argumentation-framework prompt for experiment-evaluation questions. The model needs to extract not only arguments, but also the meaning and direction of the measured variable. Here, the key missing representation was: `higher ⓐ = stronger candidate-characteristic influence`, while `lower ⓐ = stronger policy-factor influence`. A useful prompt improvement would be to ask the model, before constructing attack relations, to define every experimental variable and state what increase/decrease in that variable would imply for the hypothesis.
