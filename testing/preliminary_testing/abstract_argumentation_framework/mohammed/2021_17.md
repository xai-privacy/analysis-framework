## 3. Gold Answer and Original Rationale

```text
Gold answer: 3
```

**preliminaries:** The passages excerpt the core of the classic debate between Clifford and James. Clifford argues that it is wrong to form beliefs based on insufficient evidence. His argument is that, if one comes to form beliefs on insufficient evidence, society becomes more susceptible to being deceived, regressing to a barbaric state as a result.James emphasizes that the pursuit of truth and the avoidance of error are distinct intellectual tasks, pointing out that Clifford’s stance is justifiable only if one prioritizes the avoidance of error—and that the choice between the two is ultimately a matter of sentiment, not evidence.

**statement_1_rationale:** (a) Alice’s conclusion is "To believe something on insufficient evidence is always, everywhere, and for anyone, wrong." She justifies this by arguing that not following this leads society into a 'state of barbarism.' Thus, (a) is a correct analysis.

**statement_2_rationale:** (b) Bob argues that there are two epistemic duties, "Believe the truth!" and "Avoid error!", and that Clifford focuses only on the latter. He also claims that this choice is grounded not in evidence but in sentiment. Therefore, according to Bob, Clifford’s belief in ⓐ is not based on sufficient evidence. Thus, (b) is a correct analysis.

**statement_3_rationale:** (c) Bob’s argument is unrelated to the claim that "even beliefs based on sufficient evidence can be mistaken." Even if that statement is false, that is, even if beliefs based on sufficient evidence could never be mistaken, Bob’s argument remains unaffected. He merely points out that the disposition to base belief only on sufficient evidence is itself grounded in sentiment. Therefore, (c) is not a correct analysis.

**answer:** Since only (a) and (b) are correct, the answer is ③.

## 14. Argumentation Framework Result Analysis

- **Predicted answer:** 1
- **Gold answer:** 3
- **Correct or incorrect:** Incorrect.
- **Did argument extraction help?** Partially, but not enough. The extraction identified the broad Clifford/James disagreement about belief, evidence, truth, and error, but it distorted Alice's position by saying her conclusion was that it is “not wrong” to hold a belief on insufficient evidence. In the source passage and gold rationale, Alice/Clifford's conclusion is the opposite: believing on insufficient evidence is always wrong.
- **Did attack-relation extraction help?** Not reliably. The attack graph introduced confusing and partly inconsistent relations. It treated Alice, Clifford, and James as separate argument nodes even though Alice presents Clifford's position and Bob presents James's position. It also produced a cyclic graph, including Clifford → James and James → Clifford, despite the prompt asking for a directed acyclic graph.
- **Main remaining issue:** The framework helped the model reject statement (c), which the baseline had incorrectly accepted, but it still missed statement (b). The model failed to connect Bob/James's claim that Clifford's stance is based on sentiment rather than evidence to the conclusion that Clifford's belief in ⓐ is not based on sufficient evidence. The final response also does not end in the required exact format `Answer: <number>`.

## 15. Baseline vs Argumentation Framework Comparison

- **Baseline outcome:** Incorrect. The baseline selected option ④ instead of the gold answer ③.
- **Argumentation-framework outcome:** Incorrect. The argumentation-framework run selected option ① instead of the gold answer ③.
- **Improved / worse / inconclusive:** Inconclusive, with a small reasoning improvement but no final-answer improvement.

Both approaches failed to reach the gold answer. The baseline understood statement (a), but it missed Bob/James's evidence-versus-sentiment point in statement (b) and incorrectly treated statement (c) as necessary to Bob's argument. The argumentation-framework approach corrected one part of the reasoning by rejecting statement (c), but it still failed to identify statement (b) as correct. The intermediate argument extraction also introduced errors by separating Alice from Clifford and Bob from James too sharply, which weakened the later evaluation step.

## 16. Final Notes

This example shows that the three-step argumentation framework can help remove one wrong option, but only if the first extraction step preserves the speaker/source mapping correctly. The model treated Alice, Clifford, Bob, and James as separate argumentative agents instead of recognizing that Alice is presenting Clifford's view and Bob is presenting James's response. That caused the attack graph to become confused and cyclic. A useful prompt improvement may be to ask the model first to identify “who is presenting whose position” before extracting arguments and attack relations.
