# Question 2023_13

## Original Question

Risks may sometimes materialize and sometimes not. Investing in stocks involves the risk of losing the principal, and in reality, there are occasions when this risk is realized and principal loss occurs, and times when it does not. The kind of risk that does not materialize, as in the latter case, is called “pure risk.” Alice, Bob, Charlie, and Diane are having a debate about whether an act that only imposes pure risk on others is morally wrong.Alice: An act that imposes risk on others is, in itself, morally wrong regardless of whether the risk materializes. Anyone who is exposed to a risk inevitably suffers some harm. Think of a passenger riding on a plane with a risk of crashing due to poor maintenance. Even if the risk of a crash does not materialize and the person was unaware of the risk, you can still say the passenger was harmed merely by being exposed to a life-threatening situation for an extended period.Bob: But it is wrong to indiscriminately condemn acts that impose pure risk as morally wrong. The only cases in which such acts can be judged morally wrong are those in which, had the person known about the risk, their autonomous choice of action could have been different.Charlie: That’s not right. If you limit the scope in that way, you’ll have a lot of cases that go against our intuition. Sometimes, imposing pure risk on people who are in a coma or on infants can also be morally wrong. But such people lack the capacity for autonomous choice in the first place, so there wouldn’t be any change in their decisions, would there?Diane: I have a different view. Imagine a car that runs a red light, and a pedestrian narrowly avoids being hit. Even though the risk of a traffic accident did not materialize, the pedestrian could have experienced considerable psychological trauma. In cases of pure risk, I think we should only judge the act as morally wrong when some kind of incidental harm has actually occurred, as in this scenario. <question> Of the following analyses regarding the debate, which combination is/are correct according to the <statements> below? <statements> (a) Alice and Charlie both acknowledge that imposing pure risk on a person in a coma can be morally wrong. (b) If Bob or Diane judge some act imposing pure risk to be morally wrong, Alice would also judge it as such. (c) Bob and Charlie have different opinions regarding whether imposing pure risk that infringes another person’s autonomy is morally wrong. <choices> ① (a) ② (c) ③ (a), (b) ④ (b), (c) ⑤ (a), (b), (c)

## Original Answer

**Answer choice:** 3

Therefore, only (a) and (b) are correct analyses, so the answer is ③.

## LLM Response

Answer-{④}

Rationale 1. Diane’s statement suggests that imposing pure risk on someone who has not been harmed (e.g., a pedestrian who narrowly avoids being hit by a car running a red light) should not be considered morally wrong, unless some kind of incidental harm has occurred.

2. Charlie’s statement states that if you limit the scope in that way (i.e., considering only cases where a person's autonomous choice of action could have been different), you'll have a lot of cases that go against our intuition. This implies that Charlie does not agree with Alice that all acts imposing pure risk are morally wrong.

3. Since Diane’s statement supports the idea that imposing pure risk on someone who has not been harmed should not be morally wrong, option (b) cannot be true. If Bob or Diane judge some act imposing pure risk to be morally wrong, it does not necessarily mean that Alice would also judge it as such.

4. Given that Charlie disagrees with Alice, and Diane supports a different view, option (c) is correct. Bob and Charlie have different opinions regarding whether imposing pure risk that infringes another person's autonomy is morally wrong.
