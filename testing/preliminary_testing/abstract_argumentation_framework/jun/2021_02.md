## Question

> \<Debate\> Country X’s "Criminal Act" prohibits the production and distribution of obscene materials, while the "Copyright Act" protects creative works expressing human thoughts or emotions as copyrighted works for the enhancement and development of culture and related industries. There is debate about whether obscene materials should be protected as copyrighted works under the "Copyright Act".Alice: The "Copyright Act" only requires creativity as a condition for a work, and does not demand morality. In order to encourage creation and promote cultural diversity, the recognition of a work should be value-neutral.Bob: While the "Criminal Act" prohibits the production and distribution of obscene materials, protecting the resulting obscene materials as copyrighted works effectively grants rights to those with "dirty hands" who have committed illegal acts, and recognizes property value and protection for the fruits of illegal conduct, which should not be realized as property rights. This contradicts the principles of legal unity and fairness.Charlie: Clearly socially harmful obscene materials—such as child pornography or videos recording actual rape—should not be recognized as works, but for other types of obscene materials, recognizing them as works can help minimize the infringement on freedom of expression and property rights caused by regulation of obscene materials. \<question\> Which of the following \<statements\> correctly analyzes the \<debate\> above?\<statements\> (a) Alice presupposes that creativity cannot be acknowledged for obscene forms of expression.(b) Bob does not regard murals painted in legally prohibited locations or works that incite the public in violation of the National Security Act as objects of protection under the Copyright Act.(c) Charlie presupposes that even within the same era and region, the legal evaluation of obscenity may vary depending on the purpose, method, and audience of distribution. \<choices\> ① (a) ② (b) ③ (a), (c) ④ (b), (c) ⑤ (a), (b), (c)

**Statements**

> (a) Alice presupposes that creativity cannot be acknowledged for obscene forms of expression.
>
> (b) Bob does not regard murals painted in legally prohibited locations or works that incite the public in violation of the National Security Act as objects of protection under the Copyright Act.
>
> (c) Charlie presupposes that even within the same era and region, the legal evaluation of obscenity may vary depending on the purpose, method, and audience of distribution.

## Original Rationale

**Preliminaries**

Alice, Bob, and Charlie are debating whether obscene materials should be protected as copyrighted works under the "Copyright Act". Alice argues that value neutrality is necessary in determining whether something is a copyrighted work and presents "creativity" as the sole criterion, believing that obscene materials can be included as copyrighted works. In contrast, Bob emphasizes the unity and fairness of the law and includes "legality" as a requirement for copyrighted works, thereby asserting that obscene materials cannot be recognized as works. Meanwhile, Charlie suggests a compromised stance that obscene materials should be differentiated in copyright protection based on the standard of "social harmfulness". \<choices\>

**Statement (a) Rationale**

(a) Alice’s position is to exclude moral standards such as obscenity from the concept or determination criteria of a work and to use creativity as the determining criterion. This presupposes that creativity can indeed be acknowledged for expressions such as obscene materials, where morality is called into question. Thus, (a) is not a correct analysis.

**Statement (b) Rationale**

(b) Bob’s position, based on the "dirty hands" theory and the principles of legal unity and fairness, sees legality as an essential element of the concept of a work. Therefore, according to Bob, murals painted in legally prohibited places or works that incite the public in violation of the National Security Act, due to their illegality, cannot be regarded as copyrighted works under the Copyright Act. (b) is a correct analysis.

**Statement (c) Rationale**

(c) Charlie takes a compromised stance, seeking to differentiate the protection of copyrighted works by classifying obscene materials according to the criterion of social harmfulness. In this context, the legal evaluation he refers to is that of "authorship", not "obscenity", and is affected by social harmfulness. Thus, the assertion that legal evaluation of obscenity changes depending on purpose, method, or audience is not necessarily a presupposition of Charlie’s argument. Even if the legal evaluation of obscenity does not change according to such factors, Charlie’s argument still stands. Therefore, (c) is not a correct analysis.

**Answer**

Only (b) is the correct analysis among the \<choices\>, so the answer is ②.

Both approaches miss the key. The framework lands closer only because it drops (a), and it drops it with no reasoning attached. Where it does gain is on (b): it reaches the key’s actual ground, illegality as a bar to authorship, while the baseline assigns Charlie’s example to Bob. On this question the extra structure changed which statements were selected without changing how they were judged.
