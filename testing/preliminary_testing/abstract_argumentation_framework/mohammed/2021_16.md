## 3. Gold Answer and Original Rationale

```text
Gold answer: 2
```

**preliminaries:** This question concerns a debate over whether aesthetic properties—using the example of "elegance"—are real properties belonging to an object such as Chopin's nocturnes, or not. Alice's view is that aesthetic properties are not real properties of an object, and that attributing them is merely an expression of individual subjective impressions. Bob, in contrast, argues that aesthetic properties are real properties of the object, which can be perceived by listeners possessing the appropriate musical sensitivity under normal conditions. Meanwhile, Charlie contends that, although aesthetic properties are not simply a matter of individual subjective impressions, they are not genuine properties of the object either, and should be understood as being perceived differently depending on the shared musical sensitivity within each group. The question asks you to clearly recognize these differences in perspectives and to deduce their respective implications.

**statement_1_rationale:** (a) Bob claims that only those with appropriate musical sensitivity can perceive the elegance of the musical work. Since this means only that the group who can perceive elegance is limited to those with appropriate musical sensitivity, it is compatible with the idea that the size of this group could be larger or smaller depending on the era or culture. Thus, saying Bob would oppose this claim is an incorrect analysis.

**statement_2_rationale:** (b) Charlie maintains that people react differently to Chopin's nocturnes because the shared musical sensitivity of the groups they belong to is different. According to this explanation, people who find the nocturnes boring would indeed have musical sensitivities different from those who find them elegant. Therefore, (b) is also incorrect.

**statement_3_rationale:** (c) For Bob, only those with suitable musical sensitivity can perceive the genuine property of elegance in Chopin's nocturnes, so he can accept statements by people with such sensitivity that "Chopin's nocturnes are elegant." Meanwhile, for Charlie, even if elegance need not be a real property of Chopin's nocturnes, it is enough if there is a group sharing the relevant musical sensitivity who feel the music is elegant; thus, Charlie too can accept a group member's assertion that "Chopin's nocturnes are elegant." Therefore, (c) is a correct analysis.

**answer:** Since only (c) is a correct analysis, the answer is ②.

## 16. Final Notes

This example shows two useful failure modes for team discussion. First, the argument extraction step can incorrectly attribute one speaker's claim to another speaker; here, Charlie was described as accepting something closer to Bob's "appropriate musical sensitivity" view. Second, the attack-relation prompt produced noisy and partly cyclic relations even though it requested a DAG, which suggests that the model may need a clearer definition of what counts as an attack versus mere disagreement or thematic relation. A possible prompt improvement is to ask the model to distinguish: (1) each speaker's positive thesis, (2) which earlier claim each speaker directly rejects, and (3) which claims are compatible despite different explanations. For this question, the key compatibility point is that Bob's realism about elegance does not exclude cultural or historical variation in who has the relevant sensitivity.
