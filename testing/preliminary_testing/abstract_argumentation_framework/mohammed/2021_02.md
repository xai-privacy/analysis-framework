## 2. LEET-Arg Question

```text
<Debate> Country X’s "Criminal Act" prohibits the production and distribution of obscene materials, while the "Copyright Act" protects creative works expressing human thoughts or emotions as copyrighted works for the enhancement and development of culture and related industries. There is debate about whether obscene materials should be protected as copyrighted works under the "Copyright Act".Alice: The "Copyright Act" only requires creativity as a condition for a work, and does not demand morality. In order to encourage creation and promote cultural diversity, the recognition of a work should be value-neutral.Bob: While the "Criminal Act" prohibits the production and distribution of obscene materials, protecting the resulting obscene materials as copyrighted works effectively grants rights to those with "dirty hands" who have committed illegal acts, and recognizes property value and protection for the fruits of illegal conduct, which should not be realized as property rights. This contradicts the principles of legal unity and fairness.Charlie: Clearly socially harmful obscene materials—such as child pornography or videos recording actual rape—should not be recognized as works, but for other types of obscene materials, recognizing them as works can help minimize the infringement on freedom of expression and property rights caused by regulation of obscene materials. <question> Which of the following <statements> correctly analyzes the <debate> above?<statements> (a) Alice presupposes that creativity cannot be acknowledged for obscene forms of expression.(b) Bob does not regard murals painted in legally prohibited locations or works that incite the public in violation of the National Security Act as objects of protection under the Copyright Act.(c) Charlie presupposes that even within the same era and region, the legal evaluation of obscenity may vary depending on the purpose, method, and audience of distribution. <choices> ① (a) ② (b) ③ (a), (c) ④ (b), (c) ⑤ (a), (b), (c)
```

## 3. Gold Answer and Original Rationale

```text
Gold answer: 2
```

**preliminaries:** Alice, Bob, and Charlie are debating whether obscene materials should be protected as copyrighted works under the "Copyright Act". Alice argues that value neutrality is necessary in determining whether something is a copyrighted work and presents "creativity" as the sole criterion, believing that obscene materials can be included as copyrighted works. In contrast, Bob emphasizes the unity and fairness of the law and includes "legality" as a requirement for copyrighted works, thereby asserting that obscene materials cannot be recognized as works. Meanwhile, Charlie suggests a compromised stance that obscene materials should be differentiated in copyright protection based on the standard of "social harmfulness". <choices>

**statement_1_rationale:** (a) Alice’s position is to exclude moral standards such as obscenity from the concept or determination criteria of a work and to use creativity as the determining criterion. This presupposes that creativity can indeed be acknowledged for expressions such as obscene materials, where morality is called into question. Thus, (a) is not a correct analysis.

**statement_2_rationale:** (b) Bob’s position, based on the "dirty hands" theory and the principles of legal unity and fairness, sees legality as an essential element of the concept of a work. Therefore, according to Bob, murals painted in legally prohibited places or works that incite the public in violation of the National Security Act, due to their illegality, cannot be regarded as copyrighted works under the Copyright Act. (b) is a correct analysis.

**statement_3_rationale:** (c) Charlie takes a compromised stance, seeking to differentiate the protection of copyrighted works by classifying obscene materials according to the criterion of social harmfulness. In this context, the legal evaluation he refers to is that of "authorship", not "obscenity", and is affected by social harmfulness. Thus, the assertion that legal evaluation of obscenity changes depending on purpose, method, or audience is not necessarily a presupposition of Charlie’s argument. Even if the legal evaluation of obscenity does not change according to such factors, Charlie’s argument still stands. Therefore, (c) is not a correct analysis.

**answer:** Only (b) is the correct analysis among the <choices>, so the answer is ②.

## 6. Baseline Model Response

```text
The correct answer is:

④ (b), (c)

Explanation:

(a) is incorrect because Alice's statement does not presuppose that creativity cannot be acknowledged for obscene forms of expression. Instead, she argues that the "Copyright Act" should be 
value-neutral and only require creativity as a condition for a work.

(b) is correct because Bob's statement does not regard murals painted in legally prohibited locations or works that incite the public in violation of the National Security Act as objects of 
protection under the Copyright Act. This implies that Bob is not sure if such works should be recognized as copyrighted works, which is consistent with his argument.

(c) is correct because Charlie's statement presupposes that the legal evaluation of obscenity may vary depending on the purpose, method, and audience of distribution. This is a reasonable 
inference, as the same work can be considered obscene in one context but not in another, depending on the specific circumstances.

Therefore, option ④ (b), (c) is the correct answer.
```

## 7. Baseline Result Analysis

- **Predicted answer:** 4 — the model selected option ④, meaning it treated (b) and (c) as correct.
- **Gold answer:** 2 — only statement (b) is correct.
- **Correct or incorrect:** Incorrect.
- **Main reasoning issue, if incorrect:** The model correctly rejected (a) and correctly accepted (b), but incorrectly accepted (c). It inferred that Charlie's distinction among obscene materials depends on purpose, method, and audience of distribution. The gold rationale is narrower: Charlie distinguishes copyright protection/authorship based on social harmfulness, not a variable legal evaluation of obscenity itself.
- **Notes:** The baseline response shows partial understanding of the debate, especially Alice's value-neutrality view and Bob's legality/dirty-hands view. However, it overgeneralizes Charlie's compromise position and treats a plausible contextual inference as a necessary presupposition. This causes the final answer to include (c) when the correct answer should include only (b).

## 9. Model Response to Prompt 1

```text

```

## 14. Argumentation Framework Result Analysis

- **Predicted answer:** 3 based on the required final line `Answer: 3`. However, the written explanation actually says (b) and (c) are correct, which corresponds to option 4.
- **Gold answer:** 2 — only statement (b) is correct.
- **Correct or incorrect:** Incorrect. Under either interpretation, the final result is wrong: `Answer: 3` means (a) and (c), while the explanation means (b) and (c). Neither matches the gold answer.
- **Did argument extraction help?** Partially. Prompt 1 correctly identified the three main positions: Alice favors value-neutral copyright recognition based on creativity, Bob rejects protection for illegal/dirty-hands works, and Charlie proposes a middle position based on social harmfulness. This helped organize the debate.
- **Did attack-relation extraction help?** No. Prompt 2 produced an unstable and partly contradictory attack graph. It listed Alice → Charlie and Bob → Charlie, but then also claimed Charlie has no incoming edges and that Charlie attacks Alice and Bob. The graph therefore did not provide reliable structure for final evaluation.
- **Main remaining issue:** The model still over-inferred statement (c). It treated Charlie's social-harmfulness distinction as if Charlie presupposed that obscenity itself legally varies by purpose, method, and audience. The final response also had an answer-consistency failure: the text says (b) and (c), but the final line says `Answer: 3`, which corresponds to (a) and (c).

## 15. Baseline vs Argumentation Framework Comparison

- **Baseline outcome:** Incorrect. The baseline selected option ④, treating (b) and (c) as correct.
- **Argumentation-framework outcome:** Incorrect. The final line selected option ③, while the explanation said (b) and (c), creating an internal inconsistency.
- **Improved / worse / inconclusive:** Worse overall.

The argumentation framework helped in the first step by extracting the three major positions in the debate, but the later attack-relation step introduced errors and contradictions. The model did not use the graph reliably when evaluating the statements. Both approaches made the same substantive mistake of accepting statement (c), even though Charlie's argument concerns differentiated copyright protection based on social harmfulness rather than a changing legal evaluation of obscenity. The argumentation-framework output is worse than the baseline here because it also introduces a final-answer inconsistency: the reasoning supports option ④, but the final line gives `Answer: 3`.

## 16. Final Notes

This example suggests two improvements to the prompt sequence. First, the attack-relation prompt should require the model to produce a consistent edge list and then check that every natural-language claim about incoming/outgoing attacks matches the listed edges. Second, the final-evaluation prompt should require an explicit mapping from analyzed statements to answer choice, for example: `(a)=incorrect, (b)=correct, (c)=incorrect, therefore Answer: 2`. The model also needs a reminder not to turn a possible contextual explanation into a required presupposition. In this question, Charlie's position is about differentiated copyright recognition based on social harmfulness, not about whether the legal evaluation of obscenity varies by purpose, method, or audience.
